# Presentation Deck Intelligence

Use this file for PowerPoint, Google Slides, Keynote, Figma Slides, pitch decks, proposal decks, company profiles, portfolio decks, and presentation templates.

## Core Principle

Presentation decisions are owned by `WORKFLOW.md` in this directory. For native execution use `../workflows/native.md` and `../runtime/presentation.py`; browser output uses `../workflows/web.md`. All implementation and resources are internal to this capability. Technical export success does not replace design, editability and visual review.

A deck template is a slide system. It is not a long website page. The product value is the set of reusable slide roles, master/layout logic, and presentation-specific design rules.

Premium deck learning should ask why the design works, not only what it contains. Study system, rhythm, clarity, editability, information flow, and emotional control before extracting visual inspiration.

## Advanced Template Learning Mindset

When learning from a PPTX, Google Slides, Keynote, or Figma Slides reference, evaluate these senior-level layers:

- Pace: identify fast-read slides, slow-read slides, rest slides, emphasis slides, and climax slides. A premium deck varies reading tempo instead of keeping every slide at the same density.
- Energy distribution: decide which slides are loud, quiet, functional, emotional, or climactic. Not every slide should behave like a hero.
- Slide economy: remove or distrust elements that do not carry communication value. Expensive deck design is usually disciplined and functional, not busy.
- Content flexibility: test whether the layout survives longer titles, translated copy, portrait/landscape images, different chart types, seven bullets instead of three, and bright/dark logos.
- Thumbnail rhythm: zoom out and check whether slides remain readable, distinct, and non-monotone as a contact sheet.
- Modular thinking: analyze layouts as reusable modules such as hero, two-column, quote, stat, timeline, comparison, gallery, chart/table, and profile modules.
- Information priority: identify what the audience must remember, what can be scanned, and what deserves slower attention.
- Emotional positioning: name whether the deck feels luxury, aggressive, calm, techy, editorial, futuristic, institutional, startup, or cinematic, then trace that feeling to spacing, type, crop, contrast, pace, tone, and color temperature.
- Presentation psychology: assume the audience sees each slide for only a few seconds. The eye path should be automatic and instantly readable.
- Why-first critique: ask why the slide is sparse, why the image crop is close, why the title is large, why a divider is quiet, why a chart has no border, and why a motif appears at that moment.

Do not confuse mood with permission to copy. The reference may teach pace, discipline, hierarchy, or emotional control, but the new template must use a different slide order, composition grammar, motif, title/image placement, and system language.

## Commercial Template Intelligence

Marketplace deck learning must also evaluate the invisible product-design layers that determine whether a template is useful, sellable, and studio-level:

- Market fit: define the real buyer before judging premium quality. Startup, corporate finance, agency, educator, wedding organizer, freelancer, crypto startup, and luxury brand all need different versions of "premium".
- Template usability: test whether a non-designer can edit the deck without breaking hierarchy, placeholders, chart structure, image crops, or layout balance.
- Scalability: check whether the system survives 10, 30, or 80 slides without losing identity or becoming repetitive.
- Visual fatigue: inspect whether accents, cards, icons, large type, color intensity, and density would exhaust an audience across a long deck.
- Editorial thinking: learn from magazine spreads, newspaper grids, annual reports, luxury brochures, and fashion/editorial pacing, not only other slide decks.
- Visual trust: look for precise alignment, mature typography, disciplined spacing, clean data, and low-gimmick restraint that makes the audience subconsciously trust the material.
- Reduction: test whether removing 30 percent of decoration, lines, icons, and effects makes the deck stronger. Senior design often improves by subtraction.
- Deck as experience: map the journey from hook to explanation, rhythm, proof, variation, emotional landing, confidence, and closure.
- Break the system: consistency is required, but a deliberate contrast slide can create a memorable moment when the deck needs impact.
- Function over surface: separate "design that looks good" from "design that communicates, edits, scales, presents, and sells well".

Premium deck references should be studied through wider visual communication sources too: UI dashboards, film title sequences, architecture layouts, luxury brand brochures, motion graphics rhythm, signage systems, exhibition catalogs, annual reports, Swiss design, newspaper grids, Apple keynotes, and fashion campaign layouts.

## Elite Visual Perception Layer

At the highest level, presentation design is not decoration. It controls how humans receive visual information. When learning from a premium deck, evaluate the perception and performance layers behind the slides:

- Attention control: determine what the eye sees first, how long it stays, when focus moves, when the audience pauses, and when the audience becomes impressed or overwhelmed.
- Visual silence: treat empty space as an active tool for luxury, focus, breath, authority, and importance.
- Confidence in design: premium decks often feel calm because they do not try too hard. Simplicity, silence, and one strong element can communicate more authority than visible effort.
- Design taste: raise the quality of references. Taste is shaped by what is repeatedly consumed; weak marketplace templates and generic inspiration lower the ceiling, while elite studios, keynotes, editorial systems, and high-end visual communication raise it.
- Subtlety: study small craft decisions such as 8px spacing shifts, line-height, crop precision, opacity, alignment tightness, and color temperature. These are often felt before they are consciously noticed.
- Invisible design: the best deck systems often feel natural rather than designed. The audience experiences clarity and ease while the hierarchy does heavy work quietly.
- Constraint: test whether the deck still feels premium with two colors, one font family, no gradient, no shadow, no icons, or a simple grid. Strong foundations survive constraints.
- Presentation as performance: separate a live-talk deck from a PDF/proposal deck. Slides should support speaker tempo, emphasis, emotional timing, and pauses.
- What not to design: know when to stop. Sometimes the strongest slide is only a headline, one number, and space.
- Why premium feels premium: diagnose the mix of clarity, restraint, confidence, rhythm, spacing, consistency, editing discipline, emotional control, visual trust, hierarchy, and intentionality.

The deeper frame: presentation design intersects psychology, storytelling, cinematography, editorial design, architecture, branding, UX, motion rhythm, human attention, and behavioral perception.

## Template As Product Readiness

At marketplace level, a PPTX, Google Slides, Keynote, or Figma Slides deck is a reusable product, not a static visual. When learning from references or designing a new deck, judge whether it is sellable and durable:

- Template as product: check editability, clear placeholders, clean master/layout setup, cross-slide consistency, text replacement resilience, industry reusability, role variety, and non-fragile construction.
- Master/theme/layout system: audit the equivalent of PowerPoint Slide Master, Google Slides Theme Builder, Keynote slide layouts/themes, and Figma slide-frame systems. Each layout should have a clear role and useful placeholders.
- Scientific visual hierarchy: identify first look, second look, intentionally quiet details, weak titles, over-dominant details, and the intended reading path.
- Grid discipline: measure margins, gaps, baselines, image positions, safe areas, card alignment, and consistency. Premium often comes from precision more than decoration.
- Frame precision: inspect each slide at normal size for element collision, tangent spacing, title-to-content clearance, safe margins, footer clearance, and placeholder resilience before judging the deck as polished.
- Reference clustering: when many references exist, group them by market and mood before extracting patterns. Corporate clean, editorial luxury, startup pitch, creative agency, finance report, real estate, wedding/event, education/course, portfolio, and product launch should not be blended blindly.
- Slide role matrix: list required roles before design and create two or three layout variations for important roles.
- Density control: plan light, medium, dense, visual-break, data-heavy, and image-heavy slides so the deck feels useful without becoming exhausting.
- Edit stress test: deliberately test longer titles, two-line or five-word-longer copy, portrait and landscape image swaps, three bullets becoming six, black/white logo swaps, and changed chart data.
- Thumbnail test: zoom out and check slide silhouettes, cover strength, divider clarity, important-slide prominence, repetition, and monotony.
- Export reality: check font reliability, image quality, chart editability, shape complexity, file weight, PDF export, and reasonable survivability across PowerPoint, Google Slides, and Keynote conversion.

## Reference Learning Order

When learning from one or many presentation template references, study them in this order:

1. Market fit: who will use or buy this template?
2. Story flow: does the deck journey make sense?
3. Slide role: are the roles complete and useful?
4. Visual system: are color, type, grid, spacing, and image style consistent?
5. Hierarchy: does the eye path clearly prioritize information?
6. Master/layout system: is the template easy to edit, or only beautiful?
7. Flexibility: does the design survive content changes?
8. Thumbnail rhythm: does the deck stay readable, varied, and interesting when small?
9. Product readiness: is it ready for non-designer buyers to use, customize, export, and present?

If these nine layers are strong, the deck is not merely attractive; it is premium, usable, scalable, and marketplace-ready.

- Microsoft PowerPoint: Slide Master and layout masters support global edits, consistent slide behavior, placeholders, and should ideally be edited before individual slides.
- Google Slides: Theme Builder is for customizing theme font, color, and layout so the presentation stays consistent.
- Apple Keynote: slide layouts support placeholder text, media, tables, charts, image galleries, and placeholder tags that preserve content behavior across layouts.
- Nielsen Norman Group: visual hierarchy is created through color/contrast, scale, grouping, and intentional reading paths.
- Nielsen Norman Group: good visual design depends on grid alignment, clear hierarchy, intentional color, and consistency.

- Same cover composition.
- Same title lockup.
- Same image mask placement.
- Same decorative motif.
- Same chart/table styling.
- Same profile/rate-card grouping.
- Same closing/contact layout.

## Figma Execution Rules

- Build each slide as an individual 16:9 frame.
- For PPTX export from Figma, the export-ready page must contain only individual slide frames as top-level frames: one top-level frame equals one slide.
- Validate both aspect ratio and canvas size for the target conversion plugin. If 1440x810 exports as an undersized slide in PowerPoint, create a separate export-ready page with exact 1920x1080 Full HD top-level frames.
- Do not place multiple slides inside row, board, overview, wrapper, or contact-sheet frames on the page intended for PPTX conversion.
- Keep design-system boards, contact sheets, notes, archive boards, and preview overviews on separate pages marked as non-export/support pages.
- Use a separate slide grid/contact-sheet page only for review or overview, not as the PPTX export source.
- Use auto-layout inside content groups: title stacks, bullet groups, metric rows, profile cards, agenda rows, rate cards, chart legends, and contact blocks.
- Keep text inside safe margins.
- Make the deck readable at thumbnail size.
- Inspect each slide frame at normal size before final approval; contact sheets can hide local overlap, near-collision, or cramped title/content relationships.
- Do not add website nav, FAQ, long CTA bands, or scroll logic unless explicitly part of the deck concept.

## Acceptance Criteria

- The deck has at least 10 distinct slide-role layouts for a serious template.
- Each slide has one primary idea.
- Repeated elements feel like a theme, not copy-paste.
- Chart/table and footer/page number rules are included.
- The design system is specific to presentations.
- Pace alternates between fast, slow, rest, emphasis, and climax slides.
- Energy distribution has intentional loud/quiet contrast.
- Layouts remain flexible under realistic buyer edits.
- Thumbnail rhythm is readable and varied.
- Modules can be rearranged without breaking the system.
- The deck communicates why every major visual decision exists.
- Market fit is specific to the actual buyer and category.
- Non-designer usability is considered before visual polish is accepted.
- The system scales beyond a small sample deck.
- Visual fatigue is controlled across long runs.
- Editorial, communication, and trust cues are visible beyond typical PowerPoint styling.
- The deck works as a communication tool, not just a beautiful slide set.
- Attention flow is intentionally controlled.
- Visual silence is used with confidence instead of fear.
- Subtle craft details are checked before adding effects.
- Constraints can be applied without collapsing the premium feel.
- Live-performance and PDF/proposal use cases are distinguished.
- The designer knows what not to design.
- Master/theme/layout system is clean, purposeful, and easy to edit.
- Reference analysis follows market fit, story flow, slide role, visual system, hierarchy, layout system, flexibility, thumbnail rhythm, and product readiness.
- Edit stress tests and export-reality checks are completed before declaring the template marketplace-ready.
- For Figma-to-PPTX workflows, the export page is validated as one top-level 16:9 frame per slide, with no nested row/board frame acting as a slide container.
- For Figma-to-PPTX workflows, the export page canvas size is validated against the real PowerPoint output; exact 1920x1080 frames are preferred when the plugin does not map smaller 16:9 frames to full slide bounds.
- Each slide passes a frame-precision audit for collision, tangent spacing, title/content clearance, safe margins, footer clearance, baseline drift, and placeholder stress before final delivery.
